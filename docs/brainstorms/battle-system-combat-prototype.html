TzDeck Battle System — Combat Simulator

Question this answers: the requirements doc at docs/brainstorms/battle-system-requirements.md left two things as "must validate via simulation before committing formulas" (R13, R15, and the corresponding Outstanding Questions): (1) how often do battles end in a draw once Power/HP come from per-card signals (edition scarcity → Power, description length → HP) instead of a flat per-tier bucket, and (2) can a brand-new Level 1 Common card realistically win its first battle once matchmaking picks each opponent wallet's best-fitting card (R15) rather than always their strongest. All numeric formulas below are placeholders — the doc explicitly defers exact numbers to planning. This file exists to sanity-check the shape of the design, not to hand over final numbers.

Bounded damage variance — revised combat design

Deterministic combat (0% variance) produced the near-zero first-win rate above. This tests the revised design: each round, both sides' Power gets an independent random swing before damage applies, still fully automated (no player input mid-battle). Compares ±0% (deterministic baseline), ±10%, ±20%, and ±30% across four questions: do equal-strength cards split their rematches roughly evenly, what's the overall draw rate, how many attempts does a new Common need before its first win (using R15's matching by Power, unchanged), and does a clearly stronger card still win reliably.

Variance + combined Power×HP matchmaking, together

Isolating Power-only matching (above) confirmed the design's known HP mismatch. Variance + estimated-win-probability matchmaking

Neither static-stat matching method (Power alone, or Power×HP) hit the "first win within ~5 battles" target. This tests matching by simulated win probability instead: for each candidate card, roll out several quick battles under the real combat rules (same variance the actual match will use) and pick whichever candidate lands closest to a 50/50 estimated outcome. The fixed-pair rows (equal-strength, gap tests, draw rate) from the comparison above don't depend on matchmaking at all, so they're unchanged — only the New Common progression and opponent-availability numbers can differ here.

Controlled A/B: Power×HP vs. win-probability matching

A properly paired comparison at a single fixed variance (±20%, chosen from earlier results): both matching methods see the identical attacker and identical sequence of candidate pools per trial (seeded, not independently randomized), across four populations — beginner-heavy launch, mixed levels, established players with few newcomers, and a small population. Every pool also gets a planted "control" opponent near-cloned from the attacker, so we can check whether the matcher actually finds a genuinely fair option when one demonstrably exists. Whatever gets selected is independently re-evaluated with far more rollouts than the matcher itself used (decoupling "how good was the pick" from "how noisy was the picking process"). Both methods can now decline a poor match rather than being forced to accept one.

Higher variance on near-clone matchups specifically

Both matching methods above heavily selected near-clone (control-pool-style) opponents, and those matchups drew 76-94% of the time even at ±20% — so the bottleneck may be the combat model, not which matching method finds the closest opponent. This pushes variance well past the earlier ±30% ceiling on three representative near-clone pairs: stats that round to an exact tie (the realistic control-pool case), a small but real stat gap (not just rounding noise), and a longer exact tie (6 rounds vs. 3), to see whether draws ever become a minority outcome, and at what cost to predictability.

Fight-length lever: rescale HP instead of raising variance

The realistic exact-tie case needed ±50%+ variance to get draws below majority, and a 6-round fight at ±20% did far better than a 3-round fight at the same variance. This tests stretching fight length directly — scaling HP up relative to Power (a formula/tuning change, not more randomness) — at the moderate variance levels (±0/10/20/30%) that felt reasonable before, to see whether longer fights let low variance do what high variance was doing before. Tested on both the exact-tie pair and the small-real-gap pair, to see whether elongating fights also changes the higher-Power-wins-more bias.
